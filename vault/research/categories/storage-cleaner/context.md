# Storage Cleaner — context

Category 1 of 12 · US App Store competitive audit · US iPhone · October 2026

## 1. The competitive landscape

The category is dominated by a small number of established apps with substantial App Store visibility. In the September 30 US iPhone Utilities top-grossing chart, nine storage/photo-cleaning apps appeared in the top 100, including:

| App | US Utilities grossing rank | Publisher |
|---|---|---|
| Cleanup: Phone Storage Cleaner | #1 | Codeway / Deep Flow |
| Cleaner Guru | #4 | GM UniverseApps |
| Swipewipe | #11 | MWM |
| AI Cleaner | #12 | Grimlax |
| Cleaner Kit | #17 | BP Mobile / AIBY |
| CleanX | #30 | TapSuite |
| Cleaner Neat | #54 | Smart Tool Studio |
| Smart Cleaner | #58 | NAICOO |
| Clean Manager | #97 | Quiet |

These are positions within the entire Utilities category, not a separate cleaner-only ranking. *(Source: AppBrain)*

My initial finding is that competitors overwhelmingly compete on the same functional promise: find unnecessary photos and videos, then recover storage quickly. But there is already meaningful experimentation around memory preservation, gamified cleaning, and broader digital decluttering.

That distinction matters. Our opportunity is not to invent a promise that nobody has ever mentioned; it is to build a distinctive, commercially effective experience around a promise the current leaders have not completely owned.

## 2. The visual languages already competing

These representative storefront images show three distinct approaches in the market, not one universal template.

- **Cleaner Guru:** white and blue, functional benefits, duplicated-photo grids, trust through scale.
- **Cleaner Kit:** bright blue, large result figures, bold commands, fast cleanup.
- **Swipewipe:** peach and rainbow, playful swiping, real memories, emotional engagement.

These are publicly indexed storefront captures, not necessarily the exact creatives served to every US visitor today. App Store product-page variants can differ.

My interpretation: Cleaner Guru and Cleaner Kit are selling efficiency. Swipewipe is selling enjoyment. Cleaner Guru promotes optimization and duplicates; Cleaner Kit emphasizes freeing space and one-tap cleaning; Swipewipe foregrounds its swipe interaction and nostalgic photo navigation. *(Source: App Store, +2 more)*

This is important because two of the three positioning territories we initially considered (speed and memories) already have established competitors. A credible new entrant needs a more specific reason to be chosen.

### What the visual evidence tells us

Blue dominates the conventional cleaner aesthetic. Cleanup, Cleaner Kit, AI Cleaner and Boost Cleaner all use blue prominently; Cleaner Guru uses a blue/purple-and-white language. These use large phone mockups and functional claims.

Swipewipe's peach/rainbow aesthetic stands apart. Clever Cleaner uses dark green, with the absence of ads and mandatory subscription presented as part of its identity.

The first screenshots generally sell a result or capability, not a narrative. A visitor typically sees some combination of a cleaner label, storage indicator, duplicate-photo grid, and bold promise.

Three immediate lessons follow.

1. **Using blue is not inherently wrong.** The biggest competitors use it successfully, and it communicates familiar utility-app functionality. The problem would be using the same color and the same layout and the same message.
2. **Real product UI matters.** A large number such as "8.4 GB" or an obvious before/after display is more immediately understandable than a decorative AI illustration.
3. **First-screenshot social proof is a real competitive weapon.** Cleanup, Cleaner Guru and Cleaner Kit have large installed or rated user bases; newer entrants cannot credibly replicate those claims. We need an alternative form of reassurance.

I would use visible product proof: what the scan identifies, what is safe to remove, what requires review, and what happens next.

## 5. The actual customer dissatisfaction

The written reviews are much more revealing than the store descriptions. I wouldn't treat individual reviews as representative statistics. But several independent apps receive remarkably similar complaints, making them useful for identifying hypotheses.

### Anxiety A — "I downloaded something free, and now I have to pay."

Cleaner Guru reviewers describe surprise subscription charges and frustration with weekly pricing. Cleaner Kit reviewers describe misunderstanding which offers include a trial. Some developers respond that introductory offers are only available once per Apple account or that a selected plan had no trial. *(Source: App Store, +1 more)*

**Opportunity:** show the price, billing frequency and next charge date plainly. Let people see a useful scan before asking for payment.

### Anxiety B — "The app thinks my important photos are duplicates."

A Cleanup reviewer praises its categorization but warns that purposeful similarities still require manual review. A Clever Cleaner reviewer describes checking recommendations rather than trusting all suggested deletions. *(Source: App Store, +1 more)*

**Opportunity:** don't treat exact duplicates, near-duplicates and merely similar photographs as the same thing. Design separate levels of deletion confidence.

### Anxiety C — "I can't see enough detail to decide whether to delete something."

One Cleaner Kit review specifically asks for better previews of videos and emails before deleting them. The reviewer liked bulk email cleanup but avoided deleting videos without examining their contents. *(Source: App Store)*

**Opportunity:** make previews and selection controls central, particularly for large videos, screenshots and personal memories.

### Anxiety D — "What happens if something is deleted accidentally?"

A Smart Cleaner reviewer explicitly asks for deleted photos back. AI Cleaner has a review reporting serious data loss, although the developer disputes aspects of the account and describes the normal recovery process. *(Source: App Store, +1 more)*

**Opportunity:** explain recovery before deletion, distinguish reversible from irreversible operations, and provide an obvious route to Apple's Recently Deleted album.

### Anxiety E — "The advertisement made this look easier than it actually is."

Cleaner Kit and Swipewipe reviewers mention ads, usage limits and discrepancies between the advertised simplicity and the work required. That does not establish that every ad is misleading, but it does identify an expectation-management problem. *(Source: App Store, +1 more)*

**Opportunity:** demonstrate the actual interaction in Meta creative, rather than promising an effortless clean that subsequently requires lots of manual work.

### What five-star reviewers repeatedly appreciate

- **Time saved:** large libraries become manageable in minutes rather than hours.
- **Clear grouping:** seeing related photos together makes decisions easier.
- **Control:** being able to deselect images or approve what goes.
- **Visible results:** seeing how many files or gigabytes have been addressed.
- **Fairness:** having a useful free experience without constant ad interruptions.

For example, Clever Cleaner users specifically praise sorting thousands of photos, reviewing groups and avoiding excessive ads. Cleanup users praise the speed of grouping and deletion. Cleaner Kit users praise its ability to remove large numbers of unwanted emails and duplicate pictures. *(Source: App Store, +2 more)*

This creates a useful tension for us: **users want automation, but they do not want to surrender control.** That is the central UX problem I would solve.

## 6. The whitespace: what is not yet convincingly owned?

Based on the 20-app audit, I see five positioning opportunities. The important distinction is that some competitors already offer these features, but very few make them the organizing principle of the entire product experience.

| Opportunity | What competitors do | What we could do differently | My assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Safety-first cleaning | Suggest photos and videos to delete; provide selection controls | Make deletion confidence, previews, personal approval and recovery central to the experience | Strongest positioning opportunity |
| Explainable AI | Label selections as AI-powered or smart | Show why each file was selected and let users correct the recommendation | Strong supporting differentiator |
| Storage recovery planning | Display storage usage and possible savings | Show a prioritized plan to recover a specific amount of space with the least effort | Strong conversion opportunity |
| Digital memory management | Organize photos, swipe, preserve favorites | Combine safe cleaning with revisiting, finding and protecting meaningful memories | Good retention opportunity, but already competitive |
| Transparent monetization | Offer subscriptions, trials and limited free cleaning | Show exact pricing, renewal dates and what users can accomplish before purchasing | Strong trust differentiator |

### My favorite is not simply "Memory Cleaner" anymore

The deeper research changes my original recommendation. Swipewipe already has an emotional, memory-oriented experience. CleanMyPhone talks about preserving memories. Clever Cleaner competes on being free and straightforward.

I would therefore build around a more specific promise:

> **Make space. Keep what matters.**

The product would be an intelligent storage cleaner in which users can understand and approve precisely what is removed.

The distinction is between a competitor that says: *"Our AI automatically finds unnecessary photos."*

And our product saying: *"We found 12.4 GB you could remove. Here's exactly what it is, and why we selected it."*

The second proposition makes the result tangible while addressing the underlying anxiety about losing important photographs.

One qualification: this is a positioning hypothesis, not proof that competitors lack safeguards. Many already offer previews and manual selection. We would have to make our safeguards substantially easier to understand and use.

## 7. Competitor pricing and paywall strategy

The public US App Store pages reveal another important pattern: weekly subscriptions are common, but many competitors also offer annual and lifetime options.

| Competitor | Publicly listed US prices | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| Cleanup | $7.99–$11.99 weekly; $29.99 annual | Multiple subscription offers |
| Cleaner Guru | $4.99–$9.99 products; $39.99 product | Multiple pricing options |
| Cleaner Kit | $4.99–$6.99 products | Several offers visible |
| Swipewipe | $4.99–$9.99 weekly; $29.99 annual | Recurring subscriptions |
| Clever Cleaner | $6.99 weekly with trial; $39.99 lifetime | Free usage plus premium upgrades |

*Sources: current US App Store in-app purchase listings. (App Store, +4 more)*

These are publicly listed products, not necessarily the prices or plans displayed to a newly acquired US user. Confirming those requires observing the actual in-app paywalls.

For our company, the interesting opportunity is to make the purchase decision feel substantially less risky. Instead of showing the paywall before demonstrating value, I would test:

Scan → show storage opportunity → let the user inspect recommendations → present payment options → perform bulk cleanup.

This creates a clear exchange: *You have seen what the product can do. Now decide whether completing the cleanup is worth paying for.*

However, Clever Cleaner already offers generous free cleaning, so we cannot claim free previews or transparent pricing are unique to us. *(Source: App Store)*

## 8. The visual competitive map

I would map the leading competitors across two axes:

- **Horizontal:** functional efficiency → emotional experience.
- **Vertical:** automated decision-making → user-directed decision-making.

| | Functional efficiency | Emotional experience |
|---|---|---|
| **Automated / AI-led** | **Functional + automated:** Cleanup, Cleaner Guru, AI Cleaner. Large storage numbers, AI categorization, fast cleaning. | **Emotional + automated:** CleanMyPhone. Clutter removal with memory-preservation messaging. |
| **User-directed** | **Functional + controlled:** Clever Cleaner, *our proposed position*. Visible storage savings, explainable decisions and approvals. | **Emotional + controlled:** Swipewipe, Slidebox. Photo-by-photo sorting, memories and interactive organization. |

*Qualitative map of publicly visible positioning, not measured customer perception. Products can span several quadrants.*

Notice where I placed us: functional + controlled. This is deliberate.

I don't want to compete directly with Swipewipe on making photo cleaning entertaining. Nor would I try to outdo Cleanup's claims about the speed of automatic optimization. Instead, I would attempt to combine the strongest commercial elements of both approaches:

**Fast identification + transparent recommendations + reassuring interaction.**

There is an additional advantage here. We can offer users different levels of control depending on the file type. For example:

| File type | Recommended interaction |
|---|---|
| Identical photos | Fast bulk review |
| Visually similar photos | Compare images before selecting |
| Blurry photos | Suggested for review, not automatically discarded |
| Large videos | Full preview, duration and size |
| Screenshots | Group by age, topic or apparent purpose |
| Favorite photos | Protected from default bulk selections |

This gives us a coherent UX philosophy rather than a collection of unrelated cleaning features.

## 9. My proposed App Store storefront

I would use a working product concept called **KeepSpace**. This is a placeholder name, not a verified available brand.

**Core promise:** *Make space. Keep what matters.*

The first three screenshots should tell one continuous story: **Find → Understand → Clean safely.**

### Screenshot 1 — The result

```
CONCEPT MOCKUP · APP STORE · SCREEN 1

Find hidden space on your iPhone.

Your storage
12.4 GB potential cleanup
[ Photos & videos | Other storage ]

  Similar photos   4.8 GB
  Large videos     6.1 GB
  Screenshots      1.5 GB

[ Review your cleanup ]
Nothing gets deleted without approval.
```

*Illustrative interface and storage figures, not an actual scan.*

**Why this first screenshot?** The category's commercial trigger is still running out of storage. So I would not sacrifice that intent to a philosophical positioning about memories. The first screenshot must communicate a tangible outcome in under two seconds. But underneath that result, the interface immediately reassures the user that deletion is controlled.

### Screenshot 2 — The difference

```
CONCEPT MOCKUP · APP STORE · SCREEN 2

See exactly what you're deleting.

Similar photos                          Review first
  [photo]  Recommended to keep
  Photo 2 — sharpest face, highest image quality.

  3 similar photos to review            42 MB
[ Compare before deleting ]
```

*Illustrative recommendation only. A real product must not claim that it can reliably identify a user's most meaningful photo.*

This is where the product begins looking different. Most cleaning-app merchandising emphasizes how much automation happens. I would emphasize that we make recommendations understandable.

Imagine a parent with seven almost-identical photographs of their child. Rather than selecting one photograph and discarding six without explanation, the app says:

*"These appear similar. We recommend the sharpest image, but you decide which moments to preserve."*

That is simultaneously a UX improvement and a marketing proposition.

### Screenshot 3 — The reassurance

```
CONCEPT MOCKUP · APP STORE · SCREEN 3

You decide what stays.

Ready for your review
Nothing has been deleted yet.

  Identical duplicates   Review
  Similar photos         Compare
  Favorite photos        Excluded

Deleted Photos-library items normally remain in Apple's
Recently Deleted album for 30 days.

[ Approve selected cleanup ]
```

*Concept design; controls and protections must be implemented and tested before advertising them.*

One product constraint is exceptionally important: iCloud Photos deletion is synchronized across devices. Apple says deleted photographs normally remain recoverable for 30 days in Recently Deleted, but removing them from that album permanently cannot be undone through the ordinary recovery flow. A cleaner must not misrepresent that as a proprietary backup or guaranteed reversal system. *(Source: Apple Support (IN))*

I would make that warning part of the design rather than hide it in a help article.

The first three sell the proposition. The remaining images establish feature breadth, retention and trust.

| Screenshot | Headline | What to display |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | "Find the videos taking up the most space." | Videos ranked by recoverable size, with previews |
| 5 | "Your camera roll, finally organized." | Screenshots, near-duplicates, downloads and memory collections |

My suggestion is to keep the imagery consistent. I would avoid turning screenshot 4 into a purple video screen, screenshot 5 into a green gallery and screenshot 6 into a different dashboard. A recognizable UI language improves the perception that these features belong to one carefully designed product.

## 11. Connecting Meta creative to the storefront

This is where I think our performance-marketing experience becomes an advantage. I wouldn't optimize the App Store screenshots independently from the ads. Instead, I would construct three coherent journeys and test them against one another.

### Concept A — Storage emergency: "Your iPhone storage is almost full."

Video hook: the iPhone storage warning appears before somebody tries to record a video.

| Step | Message |
|---|---|
| Meta ad | "Your storage is full again? See which photos and videos are taking up space." |
| App Store screenshot 1 | "Find hidden space on your iPhone." |
| Onboarding | Run a scan with the user's permission, and show an achievable cleanup estimate. |
| Paywall | Explain the premium bulk-cleaning capabilities alongside the actual identified cleanup opportunity. |

This is closest to conventional cleaner acquisition. I would treat it as our control.

### Concept B — Deletion anxiety: "What if your cleaner deletes the wrong photo?"

Video hook: several near-identical photographs, but the differences are emotionally important.

| Step | Message |
|---|---|
| Meta ad | "Not every similar photo is a duplicate. Compare the pictures before you decide what to delete." |
| App Store screenshot 1 | A tested Custom Product Page could lead with "See exactly what you're deleting." |
| Onboarding | User reviews a small group of similar photos and understands how recommendations work. |
| Paywall | "Review and clean the rest of your library." |

This would be my differentiation test. The user is not purchasing automation; they are purchasing speed without surrendering judgment.

### Concept C — The screenshot graveyard: "You saved all these screenshots. Now what?"

Video hook: a camera roll full of recipes, products, conversations, reminders and shopping screenshots.

| Step | Message |
|---|---|
| Meta ad | "Your screenshots aren't all junk. Separate the ones worth keeping from the ones you no longer need." |
| App Store | Show a screenshot-specific custom page with grouping and previews. |
| Onboarding | Classify screenshots by age or content and let the user inspect them. |
| Paywall | Offer bulk organization and recurring screenshot management. |

This could provide a second acquisition wedge beyond storage alerts. It is more specific and relatable than generic photo cleaning, although it will appeal to a narrower audience.

## 12. The experiments I would run

I would not spend months designing the theoretically perfect storefront. I would ship a credible initial version and test our hypotheses.

| Experiment | Control | Challenger | Main metric |
|---|---|---|---|
| First screenshot | "Free up iPhone storage" | "Make space. Keep what matters." | Product-page → install |
| First screenshot | Storage number | Storage number + deletion reassurance | Product-page → install |
| Creative | Storage emergency | Wrong-photo anxiety | Cost per qualified install |
| Onboarding | Show paywall before scan | Scan and preview before paywall | Net D30 revenue per install |
| Free experience | Preview only | Small, useful free cleanup | Trial starts and net revenue |
| Paywall | Annual subscription | Annual + lifetime option | D30 contribution |
| Retention | Clean when storage gets full | Optional weekly photo review | D30 engagement and retained payer value |

I would evaluate conversion, revenue and support outcomes together. For example, a "free trial" message could increase trial starts but produce high cancellation rates. A safety-first onboarding flow could take longer while improving confidence and downstream conversion.

Winning an install is not the same as winning a profitable customer.

For our D30-payback model, the primary optimization metric should remain D30 net proceeds per acquired install, with refunds, support burden, conversion and retention monitored separately.

## 13. One thing I would specifically not do

I would not promise "Recover 12 GB instantly" unless the process genuinely frees that much storage on the device.

There is a difference between:

- potential storage savings identified by a scan,
- files selected for removal,
- files moved into Recently Deleted,
- storage actually reclaimed after deletion is finalized.

Apple's own Photos app identifies and merges duplicates, and its Recently Deleted and iCloud mechanisms affect how storage is recovered. Apple's Optimize iPhone Storage feature can also reduce local storage use without deleting the user's iCloud photo library. *(Source: Apple Support (IN), +1 more)*

I would display those states separately. This is both an integrity issue and a practical product-quality decision. An app that promises 12 GB of space and leaves the user wondering why their storage bar hasn't changed will generate exactly the kind of frustration our differentiated positioning is supposed to avoid.

## 14. The final recommendation

After examining the 20 competitors, I would retain storage recovery as the acquisition promise but make control and reassurance the product identity.

**Recommended product positioning:** *Make space. Keep what matters.* An intelligent photo cleaner that shows you exactly what can be removed and helps you decide what stays.

| | |
|---|---|
| Acquisition hook | "Find the storage you're wasting." |
| Product differentiator | "Review before removing." |
| Emotional benefit | "Keep the photos that matter." |
| Recurring value | "Stay organized as your library grows." |

### Why I prefer this over an entirely new cleaning concept

Because I don't think we should require the customer to understand an unfamiliar product category. The user knows what a storage cleaner is. They know why they need one. Our task is to make them prefer our version after seeing it.

The strongest direction emerging from the audit is:

> Don't make the cleaning algorithm the hero. Make the user's confidence in the result the hero.

One important commercial caveat: this is not automatically defensible. Competitors can copy the wording and add similar controls. The real differentiator would have to be demonstrably better recommendations, faster review, transparent billing, a reliable outcome and consistent execution.

That leads to a useful operating principle for the rest of our 12-category research:

> The best positioning may not be an entirely new feature. It may be the one aspect of an existing product that customers care about most, but incumbents haven't made central to the experience.

For storage cleaners, my working hypothesis is **safe, fast decisions**, with storage recovery providing the immediate reason to download.
